What is LUFS? The Complete Guide

Updated June 2026 · 8 min read

If you've ever uploaded a song to Spotify and thought "why is it quieter than everything else?" — you've experienced LUFS in action.

LUFS stands for Loudness Units relative to Full Scale. It's the international standard (ITU-R BS.1770) for measuring how loud audio sounds to the human ear. Unlike peak level (dBFS), LUFS accounts for how our ears perceive loudness across different frequencies.

Why LUFS Matters

Every major streaming platform uses LUFS to normalize playback. This means:

  • Spotify targets -14 LUFS (but doesn't penalize quieter masters)
  • Apple Music targets -16 LUFS (Sound Check)
  • YouTube targets -14 LUFS
  • Amazon Music targets -14 LUFS
  • TIDAL targets -14 LUFS
Key insight: If you master to -14 LUFS but your reference track is at -8 LUFS, your track will sound quieter in an A/B comparison — even though both will play at similar perceived volume on streaming platforms.

How CoProducer Measures LUFS

CoProducer uses pyloudnorm (the industry-standard Python implementation of ITU-R BS.1770-4) to measure integrated LUFS. This is the same algorithm used by professional metering plugins like iZotope Insight and NUGEN Audio VisLM.

If pyloudnorm is not available, CoProducer falls back to an RMS-based approximation (dB RMS - 3 dB offset). This is less accurate but still useful for relative comparisons.

Common LUFS Myths

Myth: Louder is better. Not for streaming. Platforms normalize loudness anyway. Crushing your dynamics for loudness will just make your track sound flat compared to a well-balanced master.

Myth: -14 LUFS is mandatory. Not quite. Platforms will turn down louder tracks to match their target. A track mastered to -8 LUFS will simply play back at -14 LUFS (attenuated by 6 dB). The real cost is dynamic range, not loudness.

Myth: LUFS is the only metric that matters. No. True peak, dynamic range, stereo width, phase correlation, and spectral balance all matter for professional quality.
